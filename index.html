Lattice Gentle

SVP · LWE · SIS · eprint 2026/1098

Drag a 2D lattice's basis, step a real LLL reduction, solve real LWE and SIS instances over Fq, and watch toy-Kyber and toy-Dilithium stand on them — every number on this page is computed live.

WHY IT MATTERS

ML-KEM and ML-DSA now guard TLS, Signal and iMessage against harvest-now-decrypt-later quantum attacks, and their security bottoms out in one geometric gap: a bad basis hides the short vectors a good basis reveals. See that gap once and "lattice-based" stops being a buzzword.

What is a lattice?

Take two arrows and stamp a dot at every whole-number combination of them — that endless, perfectly regular grid of dots is a lattice. The same grid can be described by many different pairs of arrows, and here is the strange part: short, near-perpendicular arrows make the grid's secrets (its shortest dot, its nearest dot to any spot) easy to read off, while long, nearly-parallel arrows describe the identical grid and hide everything. Post-quantum cryptography is built on exactly that asymmetry: honest users hold an easy description, attackers are handed a hopeless one.

① – ③ geometry: bases, SVP, CVP, LLL → ④ the same gap over Fq: LWE & SIS → ⑤ module versions: MLWE & MSIS ⇒ ML-KEM & ML-DSA

⚠ Not production crypto — a teaching demo with deliberately tiny parameters. It shows why the problems are believed hard; it does not prove hardness, and 2D lattices themselves are easy (Exhibit 3 solves them before your eyes — the hardness lives in dimension 500+).

Exhibit 1 · One lattice, many bases — and decoding by rounding

The dots are a fixed lattice (Example 2.24 of the notes). The arrows b₁, b₂ are a description of it — drag their tips (or type coordinates) and watch the status line prove, by exact integer arithmetic, whether your arrows still generate the same dots. The point t is a target: "decoding" rounds t's coordinates in your current basis to whole numbers — the Closest Vector Problem, solved the naive way. With the good basis the rounding error is small; switch to the bad basis of the same lattice and the same rounding lands far away.

Exhibit 2 · Good basis, bad basis — the Shortest Vector Problem

The Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) asks: given a basis, find a shortest nonzero lattice point. Here the shortest vector is found by exhaustive search and highlighted; toggle between the two bases of Examples 2.7/2.8 and watch the answer stay put while its visibility changes completely.

Exact vs approximate — and where hardness really lives

Real cryptanalysis rarely needs an exactly-shortest vector: approx-SVP (find a vector at most γ times longer) is the working problem, and it only gets easier as γ grows. In two dimensions everything here is easy — Gauss's algorithm in Exhibit 3 solves SVP outright. The conjectured hardness that Kyber and Dilithium lean on appears when the dimension reaches the hundreds, where the best known algorithms (BKZ with sieving) run in time exponential in the dimension.

Exhibit 3 · Turning a bad basis good: Gauss and LLL, step by step

Reduction algorithms repair bad bases. Gauss's algorithm (2D) repeatedly subtracts the right whole-number multiple of the shorter vector from the longer one — watch ‖v‖ collapse. LLL generalizes exactly that subtraction to any dimension and adds one new idea, the Lovász condition, which decides when two neighbours should swap places. Every line below is replayed from the real algorithm's trace.

⚠ What this isn't: LLL at toy sizes, shown honestly. Attacking real 500-dimensional schemes needs BKZ and sieving at scale, which no browser demo can meaningfully show — this lab stops at the mechanism.

Exhibit 4 · The same gap, spoken in Fq: LWE and SIS

Cryptography can't ship pictures of dots; it ships matrices modulo q. Learning With Errors hides a secret behind noisy linear equations; Short Integer Solutions asks for a short kernel vector. Both are the geometry you just played with, re-coordinatized: LWE is CVP and SIS is SVP, in a lattice built from the matrix A. Type candidates and both sides of each equation are computed for real.

Exhibit 5 · Standing on the gap: toy-Kyber and toy-Dilithium

Kyber (ML-KEM, FIPS 203) and Dilithium (ML-DSA, FIPS 204) upgrade LWE and SIS to module versions — the same equations with polynomials in Zq[x]/(x⁴+1) as the numbers. Both demos below run the notes' exact toy parameters and reproduce the slides' worked examples digit for digit; both let you break things and watch the real arithmetic refuse.

What's real here — and what isn't

  • Real: all lattice arithmetic (exact integers), Gauss and LLL reductions (instrumented, not animated from a script), all Fq and Rq arithmetic, the LWE/SIS instances and both toy schemes end to end, SHA-256 hashing (WebCrypto), rejection sampling, and every accept/reject decision on this page. All worked-example values match the notes and slides exactly — they run as known-answer tests in CI.
  • Simplified: toy parameter sizes (n = 4 instead of 256); SHA-256 standing in for SHAKE; Kyber shown without ciphertext compression and Dilithium without t-compression, exactly as the teaching notes present them.
  • Not proved: nothing here demonstrates that lattice problems are hard — 2D instances are easy by design. The demo shows why the hardness assumption has the shape it has, not that it holds.
  • Out of scope: full-parameter ML-KEM/ML-DSA (see crypto-lab-kyber-vault and crypto-lab-dilithium-seal), lattice attacks at scale — BKZ, sieving, nonce leaks (see crypto-lab-nonce-lattice), and non-lattice PQC families (see crypto-lab-pq-families).
